[SCENE1]
In the last week of February 1981, a nineteen-year-old stood at a sink in a flat in south-west London and did the washing-up for the final time in her life, and had no idea that was what she was doing. There were four of them living there. There was a rota on the fridge door, written out in biro on a piece of lined paper, with the days of the week down one side and four names along the top, and the rota was not decorative; it was enforced, in the way these things are enforced between young women sharing a flat, which is to say through a combination of guilt and pointed remarks. She was, by the account of everybody who lived there, the one who actually did it, and not grudgingly. She cleaned, properly, the way somebody cleans who has worked out that a kitchen is easier to keep than to rescue. She was good at it, and she liked it, and she was not performing anything by liking it, and she had a job at that point which involved cleaning other people's houses for an hourly rate, and another job at a nursery, and a third arrangement involving babysitting, and between them these produced an income that covered her share and left something over for petrol. She drove a small car and worried about the petrol in it in the way you do when petrol comes out of the same money as everything else. She did her own shopping at a supermarket on the Old Brompton Road, on foot, carrying it back in two bags. None of this was a phase she was passing through on the way to something. It was her life, and she was reasonably content in it, and she expected the following winter to look much the same. And in eleven days none of it would be available to her ever again, for the remaining sixteen years of her life, and nobody in that kitchen including her had worked out that it was going to happen quite that fast.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
The flat was a three-bedroom on the third floor with a sitting room, a kitchen that four people could not stand in at once, and a bathroom with a gas heater that had to be lit with a match. The furniture had come from parents and skips in roughly equal measure, and one of the beds had been carried up three flights by four people and an argument. There was a sofa that everybody complained about and nobody replaced, and a rug that had been put down to cover something on the floor that was never named. The kettle was the good thing in the flat, and had been bought communally, and was the subject of the only purchase everybody agreed had been worth it. The kitchen had a table that seated three comfortably and four badly. There was a telephone in the hall with an honesty box next to it, because in 1981 a telephone call cost money and four people sharing a line have to have a system. The heating was unreliable and the standard solution was to put on another jumper, which is what everybody in Britain did in that decade and did not regard as hardship. There was a rule about the immersion heater. There was a running dispute about a particular saucepan that was never resolved. None of this is being described to make a point about humility. It is being described because it is what the place was. It was, in other words, exactly the flat that several million people in Britain were living in at that moment, and the fact that one of the four had a title in front of her name made no practical difference to any of it, because the title did not come with money. That is the part people find hardest to believe. She was not a wealthy young woman with a hobby job. She was a nineteen-year-old on an hourly wage who split the electricity four ways and kept the receipts in a tin so the sums would work out at the end of the month.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
Her working week, in that final winter, looked like this. Three mornings a week at a kindergarten in Pimlico, from just before nine until lunchtime, where she was an assistant rather than a teacher and was paid accordingly, which involved paint, coats, lost gloves, and the specific exhaustion produced by small children in a confined space in February, and which she was, by every account, extremely good at and enjoyed more than anything else she did that year. Two afternoons cleaning, an arrangement that had come through family, at an hourly rate that was ordinary for the work and was paid in cash at the end of the afternoon like everybody else's. Evenings babysitting when it came up, which was often, because people liked her and she was reliable and did not spend the whole evening on the telephone, and because word about that sort of thing travels quickly between households in the same few streets. She was not good at many of the things she was later assumed to be good at. She was an indifferent cook and knew it, and the flat had opinions about this. Her spelling was poor and she was self-conscious about it for the whole of her life, and it came up more often than you would think. She had left school without the qualifications that would have made any other path obvious, having sat exams twice and not passed them, and there was, in the early weeks of 1981, no particular plan beyond carrying on much as she was. She was not unhappy about this. She was, on the available evidence, perfectly happy. Nineteen is the correct age to have no plan, and the flat was full of people in the same position, and the evenings involved a great deal of talking and not very much being anywhere by a particular time.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
It is worth listing the specific ordinary freedoms involved, because they are invisible until they are removed and then they are the only thing you can see. She could walk out of the front door of that building without telling anybody where she was going or when she would be back, and nobody would need to know, and nobody would ask. She could get on a bus, upstairs, at the front, and look out of the window for twenty minutes. She could stand in a supermarket for eleven minutes deciding between two brands of something while nobody looked at her, and put the wrong one back on the wrong shelf, and leave. She could buy a magazine and read it sitting on a wall outside, and nothing whatsoever would happen as a result. She could be late, and apologise, and that would be the entire consequence of having been late. She could be in a bad mood in public. It would mean nothing, nobody would record it, and it would not be a story about whether she was unhappy. She could be bored, on a wet Sunday, in a flat, with nothing to do, which is a freedom almost nobody recognises as one until it goes, and she could deal with the boredom by ringing somebody from the phone in the hall and putting ten pence in the box, and complaining about being bored, which is one of the great underrated pleasures of being nineteen. She could go to a cinema on a Tuesday and sit in the middle of a row, which is the part of a cinema you can only sit in if nobody is going to need to get you out quickly. Every single item on that list has the same structure: it depends entirely on being a person who is not being watched, and it costs absolutely nothing while you have it, and it cannot be bought back at any price once it is gone.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
And then it went, all at once, in a single week. The announcement came on the twenty-fourth of February, and the flat stopped functioning as a flat within about forty-eight hours. This happened faster than anybody had prepared for, because nobody had prepared for it; there was no plan anywhere for what a shared flat in south-west London does when one of its four residents becomes, overnight and without any transition period at all, the most photographed person in the country. Photographers arrived on the pavement outside within hours and did not leave, and there were not two or three of them. They were there at seven in the morning, and at lunchtime, and they were there at eleven at night and they were there in the rain, and they knew the car, and they knew the front door, and within a day they knew which windows were which. The telephone in the hall, the one with the honesty box beside it, started ringing with calls that were not for anybody who lived there, at hours when nobody rings anybody. The other three found that going in and out of their own home now involved a decision. One of the other three came home from work and could not get to her own front door without explaining who she was. Somebody put up a sheet over one of the windows, which helped for about a day and then became part of what was photographed. The building had other residents, none of whom had agreed to any of this. And the practical questions, the ones nobody thinks about in advance, arrived all at once and had no good answers: how does she get to work in the morning, is there still a job at the end of it, who answers the telephone, what happens to the rota.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
She left the flat on the twenty-fifth. The leaving itself took under an hour and was, by every description of it, unremarkable to watch. She packed in the afternoon, which did not take long, because a nineteen-year-old on an hourly wage who has been in a flat for eighteen months does not own very much: clothes, a stereo, some records, photographs, the contents of one chest of drawers and part of a wardrobe. The furniture stayed because none of it was hers. Somebody made tea, because that is what is done, and the four of them sat in a sitting room they had shared for eighteen months with the curtains shut in the middle of the day. Nobody made a speech. Nobody took a photograph, which is the detail that seems strangest now and was completely natural then, because taking a photograph in 1981 meant a film and a chemist and you did not use one on a Wednesday afternoon. There was no ceremony of any kind, and this is normal for departures that turn out to be historic, because nobody involved knows yet which category the afternoon belongs to; it felt, at the time, like somebody moving out earlier than expected under unusual circumstances. The other three were going to keep the flat on and find a fourth person, and did, and the rota carried on with a different name in the fourth column. There was some discussion about who would take over the cleaning, and it was genuinely funny at the time, and everybody laughed, because it was February and nobody in that room had any framework for what was actually happening. She said she would be back, and she meant it completely. Everybody in the room agreed that she would be back, and everybody in it meant it sincerely, and not one part of it was true.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
There is one detail from that afternoon that is worth the whole story. Before she left, she took the rota off the fridge door, where it had been held on by two pieces of tape, and she wrote something on it, and she put it back. What she wrote is not recorded anywhere reliable and it does not matter very much, and the absence of a reliable record is itself the point, because nobody in that kitchen thought they were witnessing anything; several versions exist, told by different people at different distances from the afternoon, and they are all some variation on a joke about the washing-up and who was going to be doing it now. What matters is the gesture itself. She did not write a letter to anybody, or say anything meaningful at the door, or mark the afternoon in any way that a person would mark an ending, because she did not think it was one. What she did was the gesture of a nineteen-year-old leaving a flat share for a while, and which is exactly what anybody that age does on the last afternoon: you leave a note on the fridge, in biro, that is funny to four people and to nobody else on earth. It was a domestic joke in a shared kitchen. That is the last completely ordinary thing she ever did, and it happened in a kitchen in London on a Wednesday afternoon in February while a photographer stood on the pavement outside waiting for her to come down. Somebody said something from the sitting room that made her laugh, and which nobody afterwards could remember. She went down the three flights with a bag, past the doors of neighbours who had been reading about her at breakfast. The car was there. The door of the building closed behind her with the particular sound that a communal front door makes, and the whole of the rest of it started in the street outside, and she was nineteen years old and had been nineteen for seven months.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
The thing that was lost was not the flat, which was a cold three-bedroom with a bad sofa and a gas heater that had to be lit with a match. What was lost was the category. There is a kind of person who can stand in a supermarket on the Old Brompton Road holding two bags and be nobody in particular, and she stopped being that kind of person in the last week of February 1981, at the age of nineteen, without any warning and without being asked, and there is no route back into that category once you have left it. Not with money, not with time, not with distance, not with any arrangement that anybody has ever devised, and she tried several of them over the following sixteen years. She had sixteen more years and they contained an extraordinary amount, more than most lives hold, and almost none of it was boring, and she never again bought a magazine and read it on a wall without somebody noticing, not once, not anywhere, for the rest of her life. That is the actual shape of what happened in that particular week in February, and it is worth holding on to before the rest of the story, because everything that came afterwards has been examined from every possible angle for forty years by people who were not there, and the eleven days before it have hardly been looked at at all, and they are the last stretch of her life that belonged entirely to her: four young women in a cold flat, a rota on a fridge door in biro, an honesty box by the telephone, a saucepan nobody would claim, a gas heater that had to be lit with a match, and a nineteen-year-old who was good at cleaning and liked doing it and expected the following winter to look much the same.
[/SCENE8]
