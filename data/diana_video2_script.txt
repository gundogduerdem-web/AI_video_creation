In the spring of 1982, in a first-floor fitting room above a street in central London, a woman knelt on a wooden floor with a mouthful of pins and wrote a number on an index card. The number was five feet ten and three quarter inches. She had taken the measurement herself, twice, because the first time she had not believed it, and she was the sort of fitter who did not trust a figure she had only read once. The woman standing in front of her looked at the card upside down from where she stood, which is the way anybody reads a card being written at their feet, and she asked, quite pleasantly, whether it was necessary to write that part down. The fitter said that it was, because the cutting depended on it and a quarter of an inch was the difference between a hem that hung and a hem that did not. Diana said she understood that. Then she asked whether the card was kept anywhere, and who else read it, and whether it travelled with the pattern or stayed in the room. The fitter told her the truth: the card lived in a drawer in that room and nobody outside the workroom ever asked to see it. The room itself was nothing: a sash window that stuck, a gas fire that was never lit, a three-panel mirror on castors, and a rail. There was a pause that went on slightly longer than the question deserved. The princess said that was good, and then she laughed at herself for having asked, and the fitting carried on. But the fitter, who had been putting pins in hems for thirty-one years and had knelt in front of a great many women, did not forget it, because in three decades of the same work she had never once been asked where a measurement was kept.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
The fitter was fifty-four years old that spring. She had started in the trade at fifteen, in a workroom where the windows were painted shut, and she had spent her whole working life at the height of other people's knees. She had the particular authority of somebody who is never looked at directly. Women told her things while she was pinning that they would not have said across a table, partly because she was below their eyeline and partly because a fitting takes forty minutes and forty minutes of standing still is a long time to say nothing. She had heard about affairs and diagnoses and money and dead mothers. She had learned to receive all of it with the same short hum and to keep pinning, because the worst thing a fitter can do is stop working and look up, which turns a confidence into a conversation and embarrasses everybody. So she hummed, and she pinned, and she remembered, and she never once repeated any of it, which is the entire reason the work kept coming to her. What she noticed over the following months, once the appointments in that room became regular, was not gossip at all. It was a pattern in the instructions. They came from several directions and never all at once and no single one of them was strange. Flat shoes for the walkabout. Flat shoes for the airport. Something with a lower heel for the group photograph. Consider the line of the shoulder when standing beside others. Nothing in any of it was unkind and none of it was unreasonable, and taken together, week after week, all of it pointed the same way. Every instruction that arrived in that room was, in some form, about making the same woman smaller.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
It had started long before the fitting room. In the autumn she was thirteen, at a school hall in Norfolk with a sprung floor and a piano nobody had tuned, Diana Spencer had been stood against a wall and measured with a wooden rule by a teacher who was kind about it and completely clear. She was already five feet eight, in socks, at thirteen, which put her taller than two of the women teaching her. The teacher did the arithmetic that ballet teachers do, which involves a girl's height, her mother's height, and how much growing is still to come, and she arrived at a number that put the whole thing out of reach. She did not say the girl was untalented, because she was not. She said a company would not take her at that height and that it was better to know at thirteen than at nineteen. She was told to keep dancing for the pleasure of it, which is completely correct advice and lands, at thirteen, as a consolation prize handed over in front of eleven other girls measured against the same wall who had all been given different arithmetic. All of this was true. It was also, by the standards of what a thirteen-year-old can absorb in an afternoon, an enormous thing to be handed on a Tuesday and then be expected to go to a geography lesson. She did not cry in the changing room afterwards, which several of the other girls remarked on, and she was back at the barre the following week, and kept going as she got better and got taller, until the two curves crossed exactly as the teacher had said. She never performed anything, anywhere, for anyone. She was never going to. She went anyway, twice a week, for two more years, and got noticeably better at something she had already been told had no end point, which is either the most pointless way to spend a childhood Tuesday or the most instructive, depending on what you think practice is for.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
There is a specific set of skills involved in appearing shorter than you are, and by 1983 the princess had all of them. The shoes were the least of it, though the shoes were constant: flat, always flat, in every colour, dozens of pairs, the heel never above an inch and usually below, and reordered in the same shape year after year until the shape stopped being a choice and became simply what her shoes were. The rest was posture and it had to be learned. There is a way of settling the weight into one hip that takes an inch off the silhouette without looking like a slouch. There is a way of tipping the chin down and lifting the eyes up, so that the face is angled towards whoever is speaking and the crown of the head comes lower, which reads in a photograph as attentiveness and is in fact geometry. There is a way of standing with the feet slightly apart rather than together, which lowers the whole frame by a fraction and reads, from the front, as ease. None of these are tricks. They are the ordinary physical vocabulary of a woman who has understood exactly what is being asked of her and has decided to do it well rather than badly. The fitter watched all of it develop across two years of appointments, from the outside, from below, in a mirror, and she came to a conclusion she never said out loud in that room: that the woman in front of her had become genuinely expert at a skill nobody would ever congratulate her on, and that the expertise had cost something, because a body that spends fifteen hours a day arranged in a shape it was not built for aches in the evening in ways that are difficult to explain to a doctor. She altered the hems accordingly. That was the only help she could give.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
Outside that building she was looked at more or less continuously. There were the crowds at the barriers, which were the good part, four and five deep on a wet pavement in a town that had waited two hours, and there were hands to take and things to be handed and a great deal of genuine warmth in both directions. There were the photographers, whose lenses were at chest height and pointing up, which is the angle that makes a tall woman taller, and who were not doing it deliberately and could not have done it otherwise, because that is where a camera sits when a man is standing on a pavement. There were the group arrangements, where somebody had thought in advance about where each person would stand and how tall each person was, and where the thinking was never mentioned but was always visible in the result. All of it was the job. She had chosen it, she was extremely good at it, and there was no version of it that did not involve being measured constantly by people who had no rule and no card and no reason to write anything down. Inside the fitting room the looking stopped. It was a small room with a wooden floor and a mirror and a woman kneeling at her feet who was, for forty minutes, entirely uninterested in her face. The fitter understood this fairly early, and her response to it was the most useful thing she ever did in that room. She stopped making conversation. She found that if she said nothing beyond what the work required, the princess would eventually start talking, and that what came out in those forty minutes was not confidences at all but something plainer and stranger: the ordinary running commentary of somebody who had not been alone in a room for a very long time.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
In the November of 1984 the fitter did something she had no instruction to do and no authority for. She made a pair of shoes. Not made them herself; she took a pattern to a man two streets away who had been putting up with her for years, and she had a plain court shoe built in navy with a two and a half inch heel, and she had a dress cut to hang from a body standing at its actual height in those shoes, which meant the hem sat almost two inches lower than every other hem in that room. She was not asked for any of this and she did not put it on an invoice, and if anyone had questioned her she had a technical answer ready that was true enough to survive being questioned. She did not clear it with anybody. She hung it on the rail with the others for the December appointment and said nothing about it, which is the oldest trick in the fitting trade and works on duchesses exactly as well as it works on everybody else. The princess found it, obviously, within about a minute. She took the shoe off the rail and turned it over and looked at the heel for what the fitter later described as an uncomfortably long time. Then she asked what on earth she was supposed to do with it. The fitter said that she was not supposed to do anything with it, that it was not for wearing out, and that it had been made because a hem cut for a woman pretending to be two inches shorter than she is will never hang correctly and she had wanted, once, to see one hang correctly. The princess said that was the most transparent excuse she had ever heard, and that the fitter had gone to a great deal of trouble to be able to say it with a straight face, and that she should be ashamed of herself. The fitter agreed that she should. She did not put the shoes back on the rail.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
It was not that evening. It was three weeks later, in the middle of December, at the end of the last appointment before Christmas, when the light had gone and the workroom below had emptied and the fitter was packing pins into a tin. The princess asked whether the room was needed for anything. The fitter said it was not. She said she would be a quarter of an hour clearing up and would be doing it with her back to the mirror, and then she turned a chair around, sat down in the corner facing the wall, and began sorting a tin of pins she had already sorted. Behind her, somebody put on a pair of shoes. She heard the dress come off the rail. She heard the particular sound of a hem moving across a wooden floor at the height it had been cut for, which is a sound a fitter knows the way a piano tuner knows a string. Nobody spoke. There was no music, and the only sounds in the building were a pipe knocking somewhere below and a bus braking in the street. It went on for eleven or twelve minutes, and what happened in them was, as far as the fitter could tell from the noises alone, almost nothing: some walking, a turn, a long stretch of standing entirely still. The fitter kept her back turned the entire time, and sorted the pins twice more, and did not look up when the shoes came off, and said goodnight, and locked up, and walked to the bus. She never mentioned it in that room again, and neither did the princess, and the dress went back on the rail. She was asked about it once, years later, by somebody who had heard a version of the story, and the only thing she would say was that the woman in that room had stood at five feet ten and three quarter inches, in a mirror, on purpose, for eleven minutes, and that she had never seen anything like it before or since.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
The dress was never worn in public and the shoes were never worn anywhere but that room. There is no photograph of either, and no record in the drawer, because a garment that is never worn out of the building generates no paperwork at all. Both stayed on the rail through 1985 and 1986 and the appointments went on much as before, flat shoes for the walkabout, something lower for the group photograph, the chin tipped down and the eyes lifted, and the princess went on being extremely good at a job that required her to occupy less space than she took up. There was no second evening. The fitter was clear about that whenever the story reached her in some altered form: it happened once, it was not a habit, and turning it into a habit would have made it something else entirely. She retired in 1991 after thirty-nine years of kneeling on other people's floors, and she took the tin of pins and the wooden yardstick, and she left the index cards where they were, in a drawer in a room above a street, because they belonged to the workroom and not to her, and because a measurement is only a fact and facts are not anybody's to take home. What is worth holding on to here is not the eleven minutes, although the eleven minutes are the part that stays with you, and not the shoes, which were only ever leather and a heel. It is the ordinary arithmetic underneath them: that a woman who was looked at more than almost anyone alive spent fifteen years being looked at in a shape that was not hers, and that the only place she was ever her own height was a small room with a wooden floor, in front of one person who had made a point of not looking.
[/SCENE8]
