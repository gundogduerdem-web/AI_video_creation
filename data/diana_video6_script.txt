At a civic lunch in the spring of 1987, Princess Diana spent the better part of an hour staring at a bread roll, because she had worked out early on that if she looked up she was going to laugh. The room was a function suite above a town hall with two hundred and forty covers, a top table on a low platform, and the particular acoustic of a space designed for announcements rather than conversation. There were nine photographers along the side wall, a television crew at the back, and a printed order of proceedings at every place setting. The lunch itself had been unremarkable. The speeches were the last item and there were four of them, which is one more than any lunch should contain, and she had got through the first two in the ordinary way, which is to say attentively and without difficulty. It was the third speaker who did it. He was a man in his sixties with a chain of office and a sheaf of typed notes, and he was doing perfectly well until, at some point around his fourth paragraph, two pages of those notes came up together, and he read the whole of the previous paragraph again, word for word, at exactly the same pace, with exactly the same emphases, and with no indication whatsoever that anything had occurred. Nobody stopped him. This is the crucial fact of the whole afternoon: nobody in a room of two hundred and forty people had any mechanism available for stopping him, and the more seconds that went by the more impossible stopping him became, because by then it would have required somebody to explain what had happened, out loud, to him, in front of everybody. And the princess, who was seated four feet to his left and in the eyeline of nine photographers, understood in about a second and a half that she was in very serious trouble.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
The thing about suppressed laughter, which everybody over the age of fifty knows from a church or a funeral or a school assembly, is that it is not an emotion. It is a physical event with its own momentum, and it gets worse in direct proportion to how much it matters that it should not happen. It arrives from underneath and it arrives fast. The body begins with the diaphragm, which starts doing something small and rhythmic that is perfectly visible from the side to anybody who happens to be looking at you, and there are, on this occasion, nine people being paid to look at you. It moves to the jaw, which has to be actively held, and the holding is itself perfectly visible and looks like nothing else on earth. Then the eyes go, independently, and start producing water, which cannot be dealt with at a top table. There is a specific catastrophic moment at which the effort of not laughing becomes funnier than the original thing, and once that arrives nothing can be done, because the subject is now laughing at their own predicament, which is a self-sustaining reaction and has no natural end. Everybody has been here. The specifics differ and the mechanism never does. She was, by that stage, one of the most photographed people alive, seated at the centre of a raised table, in a room where the acoustic carried everything, at an event being covered by three regional papers and a television crew. And the man was, by then, seven or eight sentences into a paragraph that everyone present had already heard, and delivering it with real feeling, and beginning to build towards what was clearly going to be the same rhetorical climax for the second time in four minutes.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
What she did was technical and it is worth describing, because anybody who has ever had to do it will recognise every step. First she stopped looking at him, which removed the immediate supply, and which has to be done in a way that still reads as listening, because a princess visibly not watching a speaker is its own photograph. Then she looked at the tablecloth, which was white and had nothing on it, and then at the bread roll on the side plate, which was the only object in reach that carried no information at all. She took the smallest possible sip of water, because swallowing interrupts the diaphragm, and put the glass down slowly, because putting a glass down quickly is a tell. She pressed her thumbnail hard against the pad of her finger under the table, which is an old and reliable trick and works by giving the nervous system something else to attend to, and which has the enormous advantage of being completely invisible above the tablecloth. She breathed out slowly through her nose, twice, which helps for about eleven seconds. And she thought, with real determination, about something sad, which is the standard advice and is almost entirely useless, because the mind knows exactly what it is being asked to do and why, and finds the attempt hilarious, and reports back accordingly. None of it was quite working. What she could not do, under any circumstances, was the one thing that would have released the pressure, which is catch somebody's eye. Two hundred and thirty-nine people in that room were available to be looked at, and every single one of them had also just heard the paragraph twice. All of them were now a hazard.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
It is worth being clear about what was actually at stake, because from a distance it sounds like nothing at all: a young woman nearly laughed at a lunch. A photograph of the Princess of Wales laughing during a tribute to a man's forty years of public service would have run in every newspaper in the country the following morning, and would have been reprinted for years afterwards whenever anybody needed an illustration of something. It would not have been captioned kindly, and it would not have mentioned the two pages, because a caption does not have room for the two pages and a photograph does not contain them. It would have been about the young woman who found an old man's speech amusing at a civic occasion, and there would have been a second piece the following day, and a third about whether the second one had been unfair, and it would have been written by people who were not in the room and did not know about the two pages, and it would have had a life of about a week and would have hurt a man who had done nothing except lose his place. She knew all of this at the time, not by reasoning through it but in the way you know things instantly when the stakes are familiar, which by 1987 they very much were. The stakes were not hers. That is the actual nature of the pressure and it is why it was so difficult. It was not the embarrassment. She was well past being embarrassed in rooms by 1987, and had survived a good deal worse than a lunch. It was that the laugh, if it got out, would not stay hers. It would attach itself to somebody else and become a thing that had been done to him, in print, for a week, at the end of forty years of turning up to things.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
None of this would have been a problem for a person without a sense of humour, and she had a substantial one, which is the part of her that survives least well in the official record. The people who worked around her in those years are consistent on this point in a way they are not consistent about very much else. She did impressions, which is a thing that gets mentioned in passing and is worth stopping on. She did them well and she did them of people who would have been startled to hear it, and she did them mostly for staff, in corridors and back rooms and the backs of cars, at moments when the day had been long and there was a gap of four minutes before the next thing. She had a great appetite for the absurd, and in particular for the comedy of protocol, which is one of the richest seams available anywhere if you happen to be on the inside of it and are not permitted to mention a single detail of it in public for the rest of your life. People who worked with her describe somebody who laughed easily and often and at things that were genuinely funny rather than things it was polite to find funny, which is a distinction everybody can hear instantly and nobody has ever managed to fake, least of all in a room full of people who are being paid to be there. And none of that is in the public image. The public image, throughout those first years, was of somebody shy. She was shy, genuinely and for years, and it was not a performance. Both things were true at once, and the second one did not photograph, because there was no occasion in those years on which a photographer was present and a joke was actually in progress, the joke having stopped the moment the door opened.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
And then, at some point during the second run through the paragraph, the man seated on her other side went. He was an official of some kind in his fifties, a man with a lifetime of civic lunches behind him and every reason to be immune, and he had understood the situation at the same moment she had, and had been conducting his own private war for the same four minutes, and losing it more slowly. What broke him was not the repetition itself; he had survived the repetition. It was that the speaker, arriving for a second time at a sentence about the importance of looking to the future, paused for the same length of time in the same place, for emphasis. The official made a sound. It was a small sound, halfway between a cough and something else, and he converted it immediately and with real presence of mind into a full cough, and he reached for his water, and his shoulders moved once, and anybody who has ever been on the wrong end of this will know exactly what that single movement of the shoulders does to the person sitting next to it. And that, of course, was the end of everything, because the one thing more dangerous than a funny situation is another person in the same situation who is also failing. She did not look at him. She has to be given enormous credit for this, because looking at him was, at that moment, the single most attractive available course of action in the room. She stared at the bread roll with the concentration of somebody defusing a device and she did not look at him, and she got through the remaining ninety seconds of the paragraph, and then the speaker turned the page and carried on into new material and the pressure began, very slowly, to come off.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
The speaker finished to warm applause and sat down, entirely unaware that anything at all had happened, and the fourth speech happened, and coffee came, and the lunch broke up in the usual way with everybody standing around not quite leaving. What she did next is the part of the story that matters. She went and found him, which nobody had suggested and nobody would have noticed her failing to do, and which required her to cross most of a function room on purpose. Not immediately and not obviously; she worked her way along the room in the ordinary manner, and arrived at him after several other people, and she told him that she had particularly liked what he had said about looking to the future. Which was true twice over, and was said with a completely straight face, and was the only reparation available to her, and was also, if you think about it for a second, extremely funny, and she said it anyway. He was, by every account of that afternoon, delighted. He told people about it, and went on telling people about it, and it was the part of the lunch he remembered. He never knew about the two pages, or if he did, he learned about it later from somebody else and by then the princess had already told him she liked that part. There was no audience for it. The photographers had packed up, the television crew had gone, and the only witnesses were two or three people waiting their turn to speak to her. It cost her nothing and took forty seconds, and it is the whole difference between a person who finds things funny and a person who is unkind, which are not the same thing at all and are confused with each other constantly, usually by people who are not very funny.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
The photographs from that lunch show a young woman at a top table listening attentively with her hands in her lap. There are eleven of them and they are all more or less identical. Not one of them shows anything at all. That is the achievement: eleven photographs of nothing happening, which between them represent something close to forty minutes of sustained physical effort that not one person in the room registered at the time. This is the ordinary condition of that entire decade, and it is the reason the public version of her is so much flatter than the accounts of anybody who was actually in a room with her: the record consists of what the cameras could see, and what the cameras could see, most of the time, was a face being deliberately held still. The two pages, the paragraph delivered twice, the near disaster, the man beside her converting a laugh into a cough, the thumbnail under the tablecloth, the bread roll, the forty seconds of repair afterwards — none of it is in any photograph and none of it was in any newspaper, and the only reason any version of it survives at all is that a room of two hundred and forty people contained four or five who had worked out exactly what was going on, and who went home and told somebody about it over supper. She was twenty-five years old and had been doing the job for six years. She had a sense of humour that was one of the two or three most reliable things about her, and a job that required her to keep it almost entirely to herself, and on that particular afternoon keeping it to herself was the hardest work she did all week.
