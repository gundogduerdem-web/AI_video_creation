In the winter of 1987, the visitors' book at a hospital in south London recorded a signature at eleven forty at night, and the name written there was a first name only. The woman who wrote it was Diana, Princess of Wales. She had come without a private secretary, without a photographer knowing she had left home, and without telling anyone in the building that she was coming. She wore a navy wool coat that was three years old and flat shoes, because heels echoed in corridors and she did not want the sound to arrive before she did. The night sister on duty had worked the same ward for nineteen years. She looked up from her desk, and afterwards she admitted that her first thought had been an ordinary one: that a relative had come to the wrong floor and would need directing back to the lifts. Diana asked where Ward Six was. She did not ask to be escorted there. She asked the way an ordinary visitor asks, and then she walked down the corridor alone with her hands pushed into her pockets, past the trolley bay and the darkened day room and the noticeboard with its curling paper. A radiator ticked somewhere behind her and did not stop. There were fourteen beds on Ward Six that night. Twelve of them had seen visitors during the day, the usual traffic of wives and grown children and neighbours carrying grapes that nobody would eat. Two of the beds had seen no one at all, and had seen no one for a long time. The princess did not walk the length of the ward and stop where she was welcomed. She went straight to the fourth bed on the left, drew the visitor's chair close without being invited, and sat down as though she had done it many times before. She had never been to that ward in her life.

The man in the fourth bed was fifty-six years old. He had been in the hospital for eleven weeks, and in that time the column in his notes reserved for visitors had stayed empty. He had a brother somewhere in the north of England who had not written. He had no children. He had been, for thirty-one years, a piano tuner, and his hands still moved sometimes in his sleep as though they were inside an instrument, feeling along the frame for a string that had gone flat. When Diana sat down, he was awake. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he looked at the ceiling again, and he said that if she was from the newspapers she had wasted her evening, because he had nothing worth printing and less worth photographing. She told him she was not from the newspapers. He said that everybody said that. She laughed, and the sound was loud enough that a man three beds down turned over and pulled his blanket up. He asked her who she was, then, and she gave him her first name and nothing else, and he accepted it without argument, the way very tired people accept things. For a while neither of them spoke. The ward made its night noises around them, the small mechanical sighs, the squeak of a shoe at the far end, a cough that went on too long and then stopped. Then he asked her whether she had come because somebody had told her to come. She said no. He asked whether she would be coming back. She said she did not know yet, which was the truth, and he nodded slowly, as though that were the first honest answer anybody had given him in some weeks. He had not spoken that much to anyone in a fortnight. She stayed forty minutes. When she stood to go, he did not thank her, and she found that she was glad of it.

She came back on the Thursday. Then the Monday after that, and the Thursday after that, and by the middle of February the pattern had set itself into the week without anyone having agreed to it out loud. She arrived late, always after eleven, when the corridors had emptied and the last of the day staff had gone home and the building had settled into the particular quiet that hospitals only reach at night. The night sister stopped being surprised. What she did instead was more deliberate, and she thought about it carefully for several days before she did it. She did not write the visits down. The ward log recorded every arrival and departure, and a name in that column would have been in a newspaper within a fortnight, because there were eleven people with access to the book and only one of them needed to want the money. So the sister left the line blank, and told two nurses she trusted absolutely, and told no one else at all, not her husband and not the matron. Years afterwards she said she had never been entirely certain it was the right decision, only that it was the one decision that allowed the visits to continue. The princess seemed to understand what had been done for her, though it was never once discussed between them. She began arriving with two cups of tea from the machine beside the lifts, one for the man in the fourth bed and one for whichever nurse was sitting at the desk, and she carried them badly, and she scalded her fingers most weeks and said nothing. She never asked for anything to be arranged for her, never asked for a door to be held or a chair to be found. Twice she arrived to find him asleep, and sat with him anyway until the hour was up. She let herself in and out of the ward like weather.

What they talked about, on those nights, was almost never the illness. He had been told the shape of what was coming and he had no appetite for going over it again with a visitor, and she did not press him on it. Instead he told her about pianos. He told her that every instrument in every house he had ever worked in had a personality, and that he could tell within a minute of opening the lid whether a family loved theirs or merely owned it. He told her about a school hall in Bermondsey where the piano had been out of tune for so many years that the children had learned all the songs wrong, and how he had fixed it properly and ruined the Christmas concert, and how the headmistress had never forgiven him. He told her about the particular sound a string makes in the half-second before it breaks, and said that it was the only part of the job he had never got used to. He told her that a piano could be left alone in a cold room for forty years and still be brought back, provided the frame had held. She asked him questions the way people ask when they actually want the answers, and he noticed that, and said so once, and she changed the subject immediately. In return she told him almost nothing about herself, which he appeared to find restful. She talked about her sons the way any mother talks, without titles and without detail. She talked about being watched. She said the strangest thing about being photographed constantly was not the intrusion but the way it made her doubt whether her own face was doing what she intended it to do. He said that was exactly why he liked working blind, with his eyes shut and his head against the frame, listening. She said she envied him that, and he told her not to be stupid.

The daylight hours went on as they always had. There were factory openings and charity luncheons and hospital wings to be named, and photographers walked backwards in front of her for hours at a time, and she smiled at all of it, because that was the work and she had chosen it and there was no version of the job that did not require the smile. She was, in that period, one of the most photographed women alive. Her clothes were described in newspapers in eleven countries. People wrote paragraphs about the way she tilted her head, and other people were paid to analyse those paragraphs. Every image of her that the world saw was made in daylight, in rooms full of people, with her name announced before she entered. The other thing she did, she did at night, in a ward with fourteen beds, for a man who had asked her nothing about her life and would have been embarrassed to be described as her friend. Nobody photographed it. Nobody wrote it down. There was no press office note, no diary entry, no arrival time logged in the book at the desk, no driver waiting outside who could be asked afterwards where he had taken her. The only people in the world who knew were three women working a night shift, and not one of the three ever sold what she knew, which is a fact worth sitting with for a moment, given what a newspaper would have paid. She drove herself, and she parked badly, and she used the side entrance by the kitchens because it was closer and because the main doors had a camera mounted above them. For a woman whose entire public existence was recorded and described and sold, it was the only hour of the week that belonged to nobody but her, and she guarded it with a seriousness that the nurses who knew found difficult to watch.

In the last week of March, he was moved into a side room. This was not explained to him in those terms, but he had been in the building long enough to know exactly what the move meant, and when she arrived that Thursday he was sitting up waiting for her, which he had never done before. He had something he wanted to say and he had clearly rehearsed it, because it came out too quickly and slightly out of order. He asked her not to tell anyone that she had come. Not afterwards. Not ever. He said that he had worked out who she was somewhere around the third visit, and that he had decided not to say so, because the moment it was said aloud the thing would stop being what it was and start being something else. He said that if it ever got into the papers, then every evening the two of them had spent would be turned into a story about her kindness, and he would become a detail inside it, an anonymous dying man in a hospital bed, a prop in somebody else's photograph. He said he had been a great many things in fifty-six years, some of them worth remembering, and he would rather not finish as a prop. He said all of it without any self-pity at all, which made it considerably harder to listen to, and then he apologised for the length of the speech. She did not answer for a long time. Then she told him she would never speak about it to anyone, and he said that was not enough, because people say that and mean it and then say it to somebody. So she asked him what would be enough. He thought about it for a while, and then he said: come back next week. She said she would. He asked her to promise it properly, out loud, and she did, and he seemed satisfied, and neither of them discussed any of it again.

She kept the promise for two weeks. On the third Thursday in April she came in through the kitchen entrance at twenty past eleven carrying two cups of tea, and the door of the side room was standing open, and the bed had been stripped down to the bare plastic. She stood in the doorway holding both cups and did not go in. The night sister came down the corridor and did not say anything at all, because there was nothing that needed saying, and after a while she reached out and took one of the cups out of the princess's hand, because it was burning her fingers and she had not noticed that it was. He had died on the Tuesday morning, early, with a nurse in the room. He had left instructions, which was itself unusual, and the instructions concerned a small flat object wrapped in a piece of cloth in the drawer of his bedside cabinet. It was a tuning fork, steel, worn smooth and slightly bright in the middle where thirty-one years of one man's thumb and forefinger had rested against it. There was no note with it. The nurse said he had been very clear that there should not be a note, and that the fork was to be given to the visitor who came on Thursdays, and that nobody at all was to be told who that visitor was. Diana sat down on the edge of the stripped bed with the tuning fork held in her closed hand for some minutes, and nobody interrupted her. It was colder than she expected and heavier than it looked, and it made no sound at all unless it was struck against something solid and then lifted into the air to be listened to. She did not strike it. Then she stood up, and asked the sister whether there was anyone on the ward that night who had no visitors. The sister said there were two.

She went to both of them. And she came back the following Thursday, and the Thursday after that, and the visits continued in that hospital for years, long after anyone still working on the ward could have said how or when they had started. The nurses who knew kept knowing and kept quiet about it. The line in the log stayed blank. There was never a photograph, never a paragraph in a newspaper, never a mention in any official account of how she spent her time, and the people she sat with were, without a single exception, people whose names the public had no reason to learn and never did learn. That was the arrangement she had agreed to, and she had promised it out loud to a man who had asked her for nothing else, and she kept it. The tuning fork stayed in a drawer. The woman who kept it never once said the man's name out loud to anybody, and so the name is not in this story either, because it was not hers to give away and it is not ours. What is worth remembering here is not simply that she was kind, because kindness is easy to photograph and she was photographed being kind a thousand times in daylight, in front of cameras, with her name announced before she entered the room. It is that the version of her nobody was permitted to see looked almost exactly the same as the version everybody saw, except that it happened at eleven o'clock at night, and it was done for people who could give her absolutely nothing in return, and it was never, at any point, written down by anyone.
