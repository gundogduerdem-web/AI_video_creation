For about ten years there were forty minutes every morning when nobody in Britain could reach Diana, and almost nobody ever noticed they were missing. They happened between roughly ten to seven and half past seven, in an indoor pool with a tiled floor and a smell of chlorine that got into everything, and the reason nobody could reach her was not security or protocol or anybody's arrangement. It was that she was underwater. A telephone cannot be answered from the middle of a length. A message cannot be handed to somebody at the deep end. A camera at a barrier three miles away photographs nothing at all, and a person swimming has no face available to be read, because it is in the water four seconds out of every five. The pool itself was unremarkable and municipal in character even where it was not municipal: a black line on the bottom, a rope at the far end that was never used, a clock on the wall with a red second hand. She swam roughly forty lengths most mornings, sometimes sixty, in a plain black costume, alone, with the lane empty and the overhead lights only half on because the attendant came in at seven. Nobody has ever written an account of any of it because there is nothing in it to write: a woman swims, and then she gets out, and the water goes flat again behind her. It is the least eventful thing she did in her entire adult life, and there is no footage of it, and no witness account worth the name, and nothing that anybody who writes about her has ever had a reason to mention. It is also, by a distance, the thing she protected most fiercely, and when it stopped being possible for a stretch in the middle of the decade, the people around her could tell within about four days.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
To understand why the water specifically, it helps to be precise about what the rest of the day was. From roughly eight in the morning she was in the presence of other people continuously, and not merely present with them: attended to. There is a particular quality to being the person a room is arranged around, and it does not switch off between engagements; it runs through the car, the corridor, the lift, the four seconds of walking from one door to another while two people wait to say something to you. Every one of those moments requires a face, including the ones that last four seconds and involve nobody important. Not a false one, necessarily, but an arranged one: the expression has to be doing something appropriate at all times, and the appropriate thing changes about every ninety seconds, and the cost of this is invisible and cumulative and very well known to anybody who has ever done a job involving the public. On top of which she was photographed. Not occasionally. The number of published photographs of her in a single average week in the middle of the 1980s ran into the hundreds, taken in conditions where the subject cannot see the photographer, cannot know which frame will be used, and will find out what her own face was doing at half past four on a Tuesday by opening a newspaper. Water removes every single element of that, and the reason it works where a locked door does not is that it removes them physically rather than by permission. A closed door can be knocked on. An instruction not to be disturbed is a request, and requests get overridden by whoever decides the thing is urgent enough. Forty lengths is not a request. It is a physical state, and it lasts exactly as long as it lasts.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
The counting was the other half of it. Forty lengths in a twenty-five metre pool is a thousand metres and takes a competent swimmer somewhere around twenty-five minutes, and the entire time is spent doing arithmetic. Swimmers know this and non-swimmers are almost always surprised by it, because from the side of a pool it looks like the most contemplative activity available to a human being: you are not thinking about anything while you swim, you are counting, because if you stop counting you lose the number and there is no way to recover it. Eleven, twelve, turn, thirteen. Lose it at nineteen and you will spend the next four lengths arguing with yourself about whether it was nineteen. The number occupies exactly the part of the mind that would otherwise be running the day's inventory of things that went wrong and things that might. It is not meditation, and nobody who does it would describe it that way, and the people who do describe it that way are generally not the ones getting into a cold pool at ten to seven in February. It is a low, dull, mechanical occupation that leaves no room for the other thing, and this is the reason people who swim every morning tend to be quietly insistent about it, in a way they find difficult to explain at dinner. She had found it early, sometime in her teens, at a school with a pool and not much else, before there was anything in her life that needed managing. She used it for the rest of that life, and on the mornings when she came out of the water and genuinely could not remember whether she had done thirty-eight or forty-two, that was not a failure of the exercise. That was the exercise having worked.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
The physical side of it accumulated in the ordinary way. Ten years of a thousand metres before breakfast builds a particular kind of shoulder, a particular kind of lung, and a back that can hold a person upright for a very long time, and it is one of the reasons she could stand for five hours on a wet pavement in her twenties and still be upright and pleasant at the end of it, which is a thing people attributed to youth and energy and which was in fact partly training. It also did the other thing that repeated hard exercise reliably does, which is to put the body's chemistry somewhere measurably better than it was at ten to seven, for reasons that were not well understood at the time and are now on the first page of every clinical guideline on the subject. This is not a small matter for anybody who is having a difficult decade, and it is a very large matter indeed for somebody having one in public, and she was having one for most of that period, in a fishbowl, in front of an audience of several hundred million, at an age when most people are still learning how to be an adult in private. Forty minutes of hard swimming does not solve any of that. Forty minutes of hard swimming does not fix a marriage or a newspaper or an institution. What it does is reliably move the floor up a few inches, every single morning, for free, and the reliability is the entire point: it worked on the mornings when she was fine and it worked on the mornings when she was not, and above all it did not require her to tell anybody which sort of morning it was, or to be asked, or to have somebody be kind to her about it at seven in the morning.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
There were stretches when it stopped, and those stretches are the closest thing to evidence that any of this mattered, because a routine only shows its weight when it is removed. Travel was the usual reason: a fortnight abroad with a schedule built by people who had no idea that the forty minutes were load-bearing, because she had never told them and would have found it embarrassing to. Hotel pools in the 1980s were small, overlooked on three sides, frequently closed until eight, and in any case impossible to use without somebody being posted at a door, which defeats the entire mechanism. There was also the simple physical problem that a public figure cannot use a hotel pool at seven in the morning without it becoming an arrangement involving three other people, and an arrangement is precisely the thing she was in the water to get away from. So the swimming would lapse for eleven days or seventeen days, and what the people immediately around her noticed, without ever connecting it to the pool, was that she became harder to read in the late afternoons and quicker to go quiet in a car. Nobody said anything about it because there was nothing to say and no obvious cause to point at; a person on a long tour is allowed to be tired, and everybody on that tour was. And then she would be home, and it would be ten to seven, and the water would be flat and cold at the shallow end and she would be back in it, and by the third morning the thing would have reset. She never explained this to anybody, and it is not clear she had ever put it into words herself, because it is the kind of arrangement a person makes with themselves at seventeen and simply keeps, without ever examining it, for twenty years.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
Across those ten years almost everything else about her life changed, several times, in ways that were reported on continents where she had never set foot. The forty minutes did not change at all. She swam through the period when she was the most photographed young woman on earth and could not walk into a shop. She swam through two pregnancies, adapted and then interrupted and then resumed, which is its own small logistical saga and involved, at one point, an argument with a doctor that she won. She swam through the years when the marriage was reported on as a national event several times a week, and through the stretch afterwards when the reporting changed shape, and through the part of the decade that later got its own chapter in every book written about her. Through all of it the pool did not know about any of it, and that was the point. That is the specific and slightly strange consolation of a physical routine, and anybody who has kept one through a bad year already knows it: the routine is completely indifferent to your circumstances, and the indifference is the gift. The water is the same temperature on the worst week of your life as it was on the best one. Twenty-five metres is twenty-five metres on the day the photographs are kind and on the day they are not. The lane rope is in the same place. The clock has the same red second hand. Forty lengths takes the same twenty-five minutes whether you are twenty-two and frightened or thirty-one and something else entirely, and the arithmetic does not care, and at the end of it you are out and dripping on a cold tiled floor either way, with your shoulders aching in the same place, reaching for the same towel.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
One morning in the winter, which is the only morning out of roughly three thousand that has any incident in it at all, she arrived at ten to seven and the pool was closed. There was a fault with the heating and a handwritten sign on the door, and the attendant who had put it there had gone to deal with the boiler, and by any sensible account of the situation this was a minor inconvenience to a person with a car and a driver and a day already full of other things, and the obvious response was to shrug, get back in the car, and have an extra forty minutes at home. She stood in the corridor with her bag over her shoulder and read the handwritten sign twice, which is a thing people do when the information is simple and unwelcome. What she did next was get back in the car and be driven to a second pool half an hour away, which put the entire morning schedule out and required two telephone calls, and she swam there instead, in a colder pool with a shallower deep end and a rougher tiled floor that she would have complained about on any other morning, and she was late for the first thing on the list and apologised to everybody and did not explain. That is the whole event. Nothing came of it and nobody wrote it down at the time; the only reason it survives at all is that rearranging a morning generates telephone calls, and people remember telephone calls they had to make for reasons that were not explained to them. She did not tell anybody why it mattered, then or later, and the people making the telephone calls assumed it was about exercise. But it is the only external measurement anybody has of what those forty minutes were actually worth to her, and it is worth precisely one ruined morning schedule and a round of apologies, every time.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
There is no photograph of any of this, and there was never going to be. There is no photograph of her swimming at all from those years that means anything, because a woman doing lengths in an empty pool at ten past seven is not a story, and nobody was there, and the one person who was there was underwater for four seconds out of every five, which is a poor position from which to be photographed and an excellent one from which to be left alone. The whole ten years, three thousand mornings, well over a million metres, leaves behind less documentary trace than a single afternoon on a wet pavement in a market town. And this is the part worth sitting with, because the public account of a life like that one is assembled entirely out of the moments when other people were looking, which means it is assembled out of exactly the hours she found hardest, and none of it out of the hours that made those hours survivable. The biography is written from the outside in, because the outside is the only part that was recorded, and the outside is precisely the part she was swimming to get away from for forty minutes every morning. Whatever else was true about that decade, there were forty minutes at the start of most days when she was not being seen by anybody, not being asked anything, not arranging her face, and not reachable. She was counting, in a half-lit pool, with a red second hand going round on a wall she was not looking at. Eleven, twelve, turn, thirteen. And it is entirely possible that those forty minutes were the most important thing she did all day, every day, for ten years, and they are the one part of it that nobody wrote down.
[/SCENE8]
