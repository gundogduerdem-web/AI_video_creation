The car came into the yard behind the civic hall at nine minutes to three, and for most of those nine minutes Diana did not get out of it. This was not a decision anybody had planned for and it did not look like anything from the outside; the car was parked, the engine was off, and a driver sat in the front seat looking straight ahead with the particular blankness of a man who has understood that he is not to turn round. There were two people waiting at the service door with a folder, and they had been waiting for six minutes, and neither of them had said anything to the other about it, because the one thing everybody in that yard understood was that it was not their business to notice. There was a caretaker who had been asked to keep that end of the yard clear and had done it. A drain in the corner of the yard was running. Beyond the wall, on the front steps of the building, there was a crowd that had been forming since one o'clock and could be heard from the yard as a sort of weather. Inside her handbag there was a folded sheet of paper with eleven names typed on it and a note in pencil about which of them chaired what. She was nineteen years old, and the folded sheet had been typed by somebody who had spelled two of the eleven names wrong. She had been photographed almost every day for four months by then and had said perhaps forty words in public in her life, and every single one of those forty words had been said with somebody standing next to her. In eight minutes the doors at the front would open and she would go in alone, and what happened in the yard in the meantime is the part of that afternoon that nobody in the building ever knew about.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
Nothing had prepared her for it, and this is not a figure of speech, it is a description of a gap in the arrangements. There had been a briefing, which was thorough and entirely competent and concerned the schedule, the names, the order of the rooms, and the length of time to be spent in each; it was a document about logistics, and it had been written by people whose job was logistics, and none of it was wrong. There had been advice, offered kindly by several people, all of which amounted to the same thing: be yourself and it will be fine. Nobody had told her what to do with her hands, or what to look at while crossing a room, or how long is too long to hold a handshake when the other person will not let go. Nobody had told her that the first four seconds inside a door decide the temperature of an entire room and that everyone in that room will already have decided something about her before she has said a word. Nobody had told her the trick, which experienced people have and never mention because they have forgotten they ever learned it, of arriving with one small prepared thing to say and holding it in reserve like a coin. The trouble with all of it was that it was advice about being, and what she needed was instructions about doing, and the two are not the same thing and only one of them can be followed while frightened. She had spent the previous evening learning the eleven names and their roles and had learned them perfectly, and what she had not understood, and would understand at about four minutes past three, is that knowing the names is the easiest part of the job and the least important, and that nobody in the room would remember whether she got them right.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
What she was afraid of was not the crowd. The crowd was in some ways the simplest element of the afternoon: it was outside, it was pleased in advance, and it wanted very little from her except that she should exist in front of it for a few minutes, which is a thing that can be done badly and still counts as done. It was not the photographers either, though there were nine of them behind a rope on the pavement and she had by then learned what a shutter sounds like in numbers rather than one at a time, which is a sound like rain starting. The specific fear, sitting in a stationary car in a yard behind a civic hall, was much smaller and much worse than either of those. It was that she was going to walk into a room containing forty adults who had arranged their whole week around her arrival, and that within a minute all of them would work out what she already believed, which was that there had been a mistake; that she was a nineteen-year-old with no qualifications and no training and nothing much to say, and that she had been put in front of them by an accident of biography, and that they would all be far too polite to mention it and would go home afterwards and mention it. This is an entirely ordinary fear and it has an ordinary name, and millions of people have it before a job interview or the first day in a staffroom or a room full of somebody else's relatives. The unusual part was not the fear. It was that most people who have it are not going to be photographed having it, and are allowed to be bad at their first day at work in private, and will not read about it in a newspaper the following morning either way. The unusual part was the scale of the room she was about to have it in, and the fact that there was no version of the afternoon in which she was permitted to be ordinary at it.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
At six minutes to three she put her hands flat on her knees, which is a thing she did, and started on the practical business of stopping her body doing what it was doing. Panic in a stationary car is largely a breathing problem with a heart rate attached, and the counter to it is stupid and physical and works: slow the out-breath, longer than the in-breath, and keep doing it while the arithmetic runs. She counted the bricks in the section of wall visible through the side window, twice, getting a different answer the second time and starting again a third time before deciding that the arithmetic did not have to be correct in order to work. She looked at the eleven names for a moment and did not read them, because reading them again would have been an admission that the evening before had not worked. She wound the window down two inches and put it back up. She noticed that her hands were cold and that this was useful, because cold hands are steady hands, and that her mouth was dry and that this was not useful at all. At four minutes to three, with the drain still running in the corner of the yard, she took the coin out. Not a real one: the prepared thing, the small sentence held in reserve, which she had thought of herself the night before because nobody had suggested it. It was about the flowers in the entrance, which she had seen through the glass on the way past. It was not funny. It did not matter in the slightest whether it was any good, because its entire function was to give her something to be carrying when she came through the door, so that the first four seconds would contain a person doing something rather than a person being looked at.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
The first eleven minutes went badly in the small ways that nobody in the room registered and that she could have listed for the rest of her life. She came in too fast, at the pace of somebody arriving rather than somebody entering, which she knew as she was doing it and could not correct, because there is no way to slow down halfway across a floor without it looking like a stumble. She used the prepared sentence about the flowers within about six seconds of entering, far too early, on a man who had not been introduced to her and who was, it turned out, the person who had arranged the flowers, so that a sentence intended as an icebreaker landed as a compliment and required a whole exchange of its own that she had not budgeted for. She got a name wrong, and not even one of the eleven she had spent an evening learning: a twelfth person nobody had listed, standing where the eighth was supposed to be. She laughed at something that had not been a joke. She shook one hand twice and missed another entirely, and she was aware of the miss, and she spent the following ninety seconds constructing a route back across the room to correct it, which she then did, and which nobody but her understood the reason for. None of this was visible. Every person in the room afterwards described her as charming, and they were not being polite; from the outside it was a slightly hurried young woman being pleasant in a difficult room, which is what it looked like because that is what it was. By ten past three she was standing at a window in the second room with her back to forty people for four seconds longer than was comfortable, and by any account she was giving herself at that moment, the afternoon was a failure.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
What changed it was not a person, and this matters, because it means it was repeatable. Nobody rescued her and there was no kind remark from anyone in a chair by the wall, which is how this sort of story is usually told and is not what happened. What changed it was that at some point after ten past three she stopped trying to perform the version of the afternoon she had rehearsed, because it had already failed and there was nothing left to protect. And in the absence of the performance she did the only thing that was left, which was to ask somebody a question and then listen to the answer. The question was not clever and it was not warm and it did not come from any impulse to be kind. It came from having nothing else to say. It was about how long the woman had worked in the building. The answer took a while, and had a detail in it about a flood in 1974, and produced a second question, which produced a longer answer. By the fourth question she had forgotten, in the specific and total way that only genuine curiosity manages, that anybody was looking at her. That was the whole mechanism. It is not a technique, and it cannot be put in a briefing document, and it is available to absolutely everybody who has ever been frightened in a room: stop being the thing in the room and ask the person in front of you something you actually want to know. She would use it several thousand times over the following sixteen years, in wards and doorways and school halls and at barriers in the rain, and it was invented in a municipal building in the spring of 1981, in a first-floor room with a trestle table in it, by a frightened nineteen-year-old who had run out of alternatives.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
The rest of the visit ran forty minutes over, and the overrun was not one long conversation but eleven or twelve short ones, none of which anybody had scheduled and most of which were about the building rather than about her. This was noted at the time as a scheduling problem and was not otherwise remarked upon, because the people writing the notes were concerned with the schedule and not with what the overrun consisted of, which was one conversation after another that had not been on any plan. The photographers got their picture in the first four minutes, in the entrance, next to the flowers, and it is a perfectly good picture of a very young woman looking slightly startled, and it ran in three papers the next morning under captions about how well she had done, written by people who had left the building before ten past three. The forty minutes are the only trace of it that survives in writing anywhere. She went home in the same car with the same driver, who had by then been in the yard for three and a half hours. She did not say anything in the car about how it had gone and nobody asked, and the folded sheet with the eleven names stayed in her handbag for some weeks and was eventually thrown away. Her own account of it, if anybody had asked her that evening, would have been a list of errors. She had got a name wrong, used her one prepared sentence in the wrong place on the wrong man, missed a hand, laughed at a non-joke, run forty minutes over, and stood at a window with her back to a room. She had also, without noticing it and without anybody in the building noticing it either, done the most useful hour's work of her professional life.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
Sixteen years of it followed, and the thing that people would eventually describe as a gift, or a natural warmth, or an instinct for other people, and which was written about hundreds of times as though it were a quality she had been born with the way somebody is born tall, was in fact a method. It had a beginning, and the beginning was not a gift and did not arrive from anywhere; it was assembled, badly and under pressure, in about twenty minutes, by somebody with no alternative. It was arrived at in a yard and a corridor and a first-floor room in an entirely ordinary municipal building in a town that does not appear in anybody's account of her, on an afternoon in spring, by somebody who was not naturally good at any of it and who was, in the nine minutes beforehand, sitting in a parked car counting bricks in a wall because she could not make herself open the door. That is the part that nobody photographs and that does not appear in any account of a life like that one, because a car in a yard is not an event and a person failing to open a door is not a story anybody knows how to tell. But everything the crowds in the years afterwards responded to, all of it, the whole of what people meant when they said she was different, came out of those nine minutes, and out of the decision at the end of them, which was not a brave decision and did not feel like one. There was no moment of resolve in it and nothing anybody would put in a speech. It was a young woman with cold hands who had run out of minutes and could not think of any way out of the afternoon, and who therefore, at three minutes to three, opened the car door.
[/SCENE8]
