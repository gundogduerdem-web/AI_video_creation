[SCENE1]
By the middle of 1986 something close to four hundred letters a week were arriving for Princess Diana, and the first real decision of every working day was made by somebody else: which of them she would ever actually see. They came in grey mail sacks and they were opened in a room with a long table in it, and the opening alone was most of a morning's work for two people, because four hundred envelopes is four hundred envelopes and a letter opener is a letter opener. The envelopes themselves are worth a sentence: lined writing paper, airmail, a great deal of blue biro, addresses that were frequently wrong and arrived anyway because the Post Office knew where to send them. The sorting was done into trays. There were four trays at first and later there were six, and the categories were not written down anywhere but everybody working in that room could have recited them, because a system that handles that volume stops being a system and becomes a set of reflexes within about a fortnight. You learn to tell from the envelope. Most of what arrived would be answered by somebody who was not her, in a form of words that had been agreed in advance, on paper with the right heading, and there is nothing cynical about that; it is the only arrangement that gets any reply at all to four hundred people. None of this was secret and none of it was interesting, and it is the ordinary machinery of any office that receives more post than it can read. But one of those trays was different. One of those trays went upstairs, and what was in it had been decided by her, in a conversation nobody wrote down, and the instruction she gave about it was very specific and did not change for the rest of her life.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
The categories, as they actually functioned, went roughly like this. There was a tray for requests: would she open a hall, attend a fete, send a message to a school on its centenary, become patron of a society with eleven members. Some of them were touching and some of them were chancers and most of them were simply people asking, which anybody is entitled to do. They ran to well over half the volume and were answered with a polite refusal, because a person has three hundred and sixty-five days and the requests alone would have filled forty years of them. There was a tray for thanks, from places she had already been, which were pleasant to read and required almost nothing, and which everybody in that room privately preferred. There was a tray for what the room called, without unkindness, the difficult correspondence: people who wrote every week, people who wrote in green ink, people who had constructed an entire relationship out of nothing, who had to be handled with real care and not encouraged, and who were, almost without exception, lonely rather than dangerous. There was a tray for anything that had to go to the police, which was a small tray and was never empty. There was a tray for anything that named a real person in a way that could become a legal problem, which went to somebody whose job that was. And there was a tray for money, and the letters in it were the hardest to read and the easiest to answer, because the answer was always no and had to be, since the alternative is a system in which the size of your need is judged against somebody else's, in an office, by staff, which nobody could have run for a week and nobody should.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
And then there was the sixth tray, and the instruction that created it was this: anything from somebody who was dying, or who had just lost somebody, was to come to her unopened. Not summarised. Not answered on her behalf with the agreed form of words. Unopened, upstairs, in a bundle, and she would do them herself. Nobody in that room had asked for this arrangement. It made the week considerably harder for everybody, it created a category that had to be judged rather than sorted, which is slower and requires somebody senior, and it meant a bundle of other people's worst news went upstairs every few days. She was completely immovable about it, then and for the following sixteen years, and she never once accepted a version where the sixth tray was summarised for her. The reasoning, as far as it was ever explained, was short and is worth quoting in the form it has come down: somebody writing that kind of letter is not writing to an institution. They have sat down at a kitchen table, at two in the morning, at an hour when they could not sleep and there was nobody in the house to talk to, and they have written to a person. If the reply comes back on headed paper in a form of words that has clearly been used before, they will know exactly what has happened, in the first four seconds, from the layout of the page before they have read a word of it, and it will land as confirmation that they were talking to nothing, and it will be worse than no reply at all. So those ones came upstairs. The people in the sorting room learned to identify them quickly, often from the envelope alone, because handwriting changes when the person holding the pen is in that particular condition. On an average week there were between eleven and forty of them.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
The practical business of answering them is worth describing, because it is the part that gets romanticised and it was in fact mostly just work. She wrote by hand, which is slow. Her handwriting was large and round, which made it slower, and she was self-conscious about her spelling, which made it slower again, because a person who is not confident about spelling checks things. A letter of any substance, written properly to somebody whose husband has died, takes between twenty minutes and an hour, and cannot be done well in a series of interruptions, and cannot be done at all, in any honest sense, at the end of a day that has contained four engagements, a lunch and a flight, if what you intend to produce is a letter the recipient will keep. So they were done at night, which is the only part of a day like that with nothing already in it. There is a consistent picture from everybody who was around her in those years, and it is always the same picture: a woman writing letters late, propped up, with the bundle beside her and the answered ones face down on the other side, for an hour or two at a time, several nights a week, for sixteen years. The picture is unglamorous and consistent and nobody who describes it makes anything of it, which is generally the sign that a thing is true. It was not an occasional gesture. It was a standing commitment of somewhere between eight and twenty hours a month, self-imposed and self-policed, entirely invisible, performed on top of a full public schedule, with no external pressure of any kind maintaining it, and nobody outside a very small circle knew it was happening at all, then or for years afterwards.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
It should be said plainly, because the story gets told the other way, that she could not answer everything and never pretended to. The money letters went on getting the standard refusal. The requests went on being declined by somebody else. Most of the four hundred a week were handled the way any large postbag anywhere is handled, by staff, at speed, with a template and a signature stamp, and anybody imagining a princess personally reading four hundred letters a week has not done the arithmetic: at four minutes each, which is fast for a four-page letter, that is twenty-six hours of reading before a single word of reply has been written. What she had actually done was something narrower and more useful than the legend. She had drawn one line through an impossible problem, in a single conversation, and then defended that line for sixteen years, which is a great deal harder than it sounds. The line was arbitrary. She knew it was arbitrary. It excluded people every single week whose letters were also terrible and whose need was also real. A woman writing about a marriage that was ending did not make the tray. A man writing about the job he had lost at fifty-four did not make the tray. A parent writing about a child who had stopped speaking to them did not make the tray. Those are all real losses, and in some cases they are worse than the ones that qualified, and their letters were answered by somebody else on headed paper. That is not fair, and there is no version of the line that is fair, and the only alternative to an unfair line is no line at all, which helps nobody and is the option most people in that position take, because it cannot be criticised.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
What the letters in that tray actually contained is the part people find hardest to sit with. They were not requests. Almost none of them asked for anything at all, which surprises everybody who is told it, and which is the single most consistent observation to come out of that room across sixteen years of sorting. They were reports, and they were often extremely long. Somebody would sit down and describe, in detail, over four or five or nine pages, in order, what the last months had actually been like: the ward, the drive back, the things nobody says out loud at the funeral, the specific and unbearable business of the clothes still in the wardrobe. And at the end, very often, in the last line or the postscript, some version of a sentence saying that they were sorry to bother her, that she must be terribly busy, and that they did not expect a reply and were not asking for one. That sentence appears so consistently across sixteen years that it stopped being remarkable to the people in the sorting room, which is its own kind of comment on the country. People in the worst period of their lives very frequently have nobody at all to tell. This is not obvious until it happens to you. Not nobody who loves them. Nobody they can say it to without managing the other person's reaction to it at the same time, which is exhausting and which is why people stop saying it. Everybody close to them is inside the same event and is carrying their own version of it. A stranger who cannot be hurt by the telling, and who will not have to be comforted afterwards, and who will never be seen again, is in that specific situation the only safe recipient available in the world.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
The weight of that accumulates, and there is no mechanism anywhere in the arrangement for putting any of it down. The letters do not stop because you have read a difficult one. The bundle is a bundle. She was, for sixteen years, several thousand people's chosen recipient for the worst thing that had ever happened to them, at a rate of a dozen or more a week, read in the hours at the end of days that had already been long, in a house, alone, with no training for it and nobody to hand any of it on to afterwards. Nobody supervised this and nobody checked on it, because nobody had designed it; it was not part of anybody's job description including hers, and it had simply started one day and then carried on. There was no arrangement of any kind for the effect of it, because in 1986 there was no vocabulary for the idea that reading other people's grief might cost the reader something, and if there had been one available she would not have used it, because using it would have meant explaining the arrangement to somebody, and the arrangement was the one part of the job nobody else knew about. She was, through a good deal of that period, in a stretch of her own life that was not easy and was being reported on weekly by people who had no information, which is its own specific experience and is not improved by reading about somebody else's bereavement at midnight. The bundle came up anyway, every few days, for sixteen years. The replies went out anyway, in the large round handwriting, at eleven o'clock at night and later, to people who had written that they did not expect one. The fact that they had said they were not expecting anything is, as far as anybody can tell, exactly why she wrote.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
There is no archive of any of it. That is the correct outcome and it is worth stating clearly, because it is also the reason this is not a well-known part of the story. The letters she received were not catalogued or preserved as a collection, and were in most cases destroyed, which is what you do with somebody's account of the worst weeks of their life; the replies are in several thousand houses, in drawers and biscuit tins and the backs of photograph albums, belonging to families who in a great many cases have never mentioned them publicly, and never will, and who would be uncomfortable to be asked about it now. That is what a private letter is for, and a letter that ends up in an archive was generally not one. The whole arrangement leaves almost no trace anywhere in the public record: no policy, no announcement, no photograph, nothing in any account of what she did in those years, and the only reason any of it can be described at all is that the people who worked in the sorting room remembered the trays and what went in them, and the people who received the replies remembered receiving them. The shape of the whole thing, stated as simply as it can be: four hundred a week came in, for sixteen years, without a break. Most got a form of words, which was honest and was all that was possible. Most got a form of words, which was honest, and was all that was ever going to be possible, and was not a failure. And a dozen or so a week went upstairs unopened, to somebody who read them at night and wrote back by hand, for sixteen years, to people who had specifically said they were not expecting anything.
[/SCENE8]
