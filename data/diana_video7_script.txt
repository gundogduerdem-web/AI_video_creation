[SCENE1]
Somewhere in the north of England in the autumn of 1988, Princess Diana put her hand flat against a corridor wall during an official visit to a residential home, and found that the paint was still tacky. It was a small thing and she did it without thinking, the way anybody steadies themselves turning a corner, and she took her hand away and looked at her palm and there was nothing on it, because the paint was not wet enough to transfer. It was simply not yet dry, in the way gloss is not dry for a day and a half after it stops being wet. Somebody had painted that corridor within the last thirty-six hours, which meant somebody had been in this building on Sunday. It was twenty past two in the afternoon. She was eight minutes into a visit scheduled for an hour and ten, walking between the entrance hall and the first of the day rooms, with a matron on her left and a man from the county council half a step behind and four photographers walking backwards in front of her. There are two possible readings of a freshly painted corridor. One is that a building has been smartened up for a visitor, which is ordinary and slightly touching. The other took her about four more steps to arrive at. She did not stop walking. She did not say anything about it to anybody, then or for the rest of the afternoon. What she did instead, from that moment on, was look at everything in that building very carefully indeed, and what she saw over the following hour was a place that had been got ready, and the getting ready had been thorough, and the thoroughness was the part that told her what the other fifty-one weeks of the year were probably like.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
Once you are looking, it is not subtle, and the trouble with noticing the first thing is that you cannot then stop noticing the rest. The curtains in the two day rooms were new; not new that week, but new enough that the folds were still hanging with the creases of the packet in them. There was a smell of fresh emulsion on the ground floor and a different smell underneath it, the one emulsion is generally used to cover, and which anybody who has visited an institution knows immediately and never mentions. The residents she was introduced to had been arranged in a rough semicircle and every one of them was dressed and upright and could get out of a chair, which is a statistical impossibility in a building of that size, and meant that somebody had made a decision about who would be in that room. Two of them were wearing cardigans that did not fit and still had the fold lines from a shop shelf, and one of those two kept touching the sleeve of it in the way people do with a garment they have not worn before. The floor in the entrance hall had been polished to a shine and the floor visible at the top of the stairs, on the first landing, had not been touched, which is the sort of detail that gives a whole operation away because nobody thinks about the join. And the schedule, which she had read in the car, allowed for the two day rooms, the garden, the new kitchen and the manager's office, and did not include a single one of the corridors where people actually lived, or a single bedroom, or the upstairs at all. The route had been drawn. Somebody had sat down with the floor plan of the building and drawn a line through it that touched nothing.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
It is worth being fair about this, because a version of it happens everywhere and mostly it is harmless. Any institution expecting an important visitor tidies up, and anybody who has ever worked anywhere has helped to do it. Schools do it, hospitals do it, offices do it, prisons do it, and the people doing it are usually the same underpaid people who will still be there on the Monday, and everybody understands that the version of a place presented to a visitor is the place on its best behaviour, and nobody is really deceived. So the instinct to shrug is the right instinct nine times out of ten, and she had shrugged at a hundred tidied-up buildings by 1988 without a second thought. That is not what was happening here, and she knew the difference immediately, because the difference is not a matter of judgement. It is measurable. Tidying is a morning's work by the people who already work there. Painting a corridor, rehanging curtains in two rooms, buying cardigans, selecting which residents will be visible and moving the others somewhere else, and building a route that avoids every corridor with bedrooms on it, is not a morning's work. It is a project, and it has a plan, and a person in charge of it, and a date by which it has to be finished, and that date was the day before she arrived. It takes a week, and a budget that came from somewhere and therefore did not go somewhere else, and a series of decisions made by somebody sitting in an office with a floor plan, and every single one of those decisions is, on its own, an admission about what the building looks like on an ordinary Wednesday when nobody is coming. The paint was not the problem. The paint was the receipt for it.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
She finished the visit. This is the part that people who have not done that kind of work tend to underestimate. It would have been possible to be cool, to shorten things, to let the temperature of the afternoon drop by two degrees, which is a thing that can be done without a single word and which everybody present would have felt. She did not do that either. There were sixty-eight minutes left on the schedule and she did every one of them properly: the day rooms, the garden, the new kitchen with its two new ovens, the manager's office and the photograph by the front door. She admired things, and meant some of it. She asked the questions you ask, and listened to the answers. She was warm with every resident she was introduced to, and that warmth was not faked or effortful, because none of this was their doing and several of them had been sitting dressed and waiting since breakfast for something that was going to last four minutes. She shook the matron's hand at the end and thanked her. Anybody watching would have described the visit as a complete success and the local paper did exactly that on the Thursday. The entire time, she was working out what she could do, and arriving repeatedly at the same answer, which was nothing. By the garden she had gone through every option available to a person in her position and discarded all of them, and by the kitchen she had started again from the beginning in case she had missed one. That is the specific texture of the anger in question. It is not hot. It is the cold, slow, useless kind that arrives when you can see something clearly and have no mechanism at all for acting on it.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
Because she genuinely had none, and this is worth stating plainly, since the assumption runs the other way. This is the thing that is least understood about that position, and it is the opposite of what people assume. She could not order an inspection. There was no authority of any kind attached to her, not one, and there never had been; the entire role is ceremonial by construction. She could not make a complaint to the council, because a complaint from her was not a complaint, it was an event, and it would have been on a front page within two days as a story about a princess attacking a care home, which would have taken the staff apart, most of whom were doing a hard job badly resourced and had not made any of these decisions. She could not say anything publicly, at all, on any subject, in 1988. She could not brief a journalist, because briefing a journalist was itself the scandal. She could not write a letter that would not be photographed. She could not even mention it privately to somebody in a position to act, because there was no such person who was not also somebody to whom her opinion would become an instruction. Every instrument available to an ordinary citizen with a grievance was either unavailable to her or catastrophically oversized in her hands, which is a sentence worth reading twice, because it describes a kind of powerlessness that looks from the outside exactly like power. She had exactly one power, and she had discovered it by accident three or four years earlier, and it was the only thing in the entire apparatus around her that belonged to her rather than to the institution, and it was this: she could turn up.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
Six weeks later, on a Tuesday in November with nothing in the diary after two o'clock, a car came into the same car park at twenty past three. There was no press office notification, because there was no press; the two photographers who would ordinarily have been told were not told, and never found out. There was no schedule, because the visit was not on any schedule. There was no police notification and no advance call to the council. There was a driver, and there was one member of her own staff who stayed in the car. She went in through the main door and told the woman at the desk who she was, which was not necessary, and asked whether it would be possible to see some of the residents, and the woman at the desk went to find somebody. She stood in the entrance hall for the whole of it, in a coat, not sitting down, looking at the noticeboard, which had a fire drill notice on it from March. What followed was eleven minutes of quiet institutional panic, conducted entirely out of sight and audible only as doors, and then the matron arrived at a pace that was not quite running but was not walking either, with her cardigan still over the back of a chair somewhere and her reading glasses on. She was, by every account of that afternoon, extremely pleasant to everybody, and nobody in the building was in the slightest doubt about what was happening. And the princess said that she had been thinking about the visit in the autumn and had wanted to come back properly, and asked whether she might see the rooms upstairs. Not the day rooms, which she had already seen and complimented and been photographed in. The rooms upstairs.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
She was there for two hours and ten minutes, which is twice the length of the official visit and was not in anybody's diary. She saw the first floor, and the corridor at the back of the ground floor which had not been painted and which the October route had gone around, and a bathroom with a broken window that had been boarded since the summer, and a dayroom on the first floor with eleven people in it and one television and no curtains at all, new or otherwise. She was shown all of it, room by room, because at that point there was no alternative available to anybody in the building and everybody involved understood that perfectly. She sat down with people who had not been brought downstairs in October, a number of whom had not had a visitor of any kind that year, and one of whom did not believe who she was and had to be persuaded by a nurse, which took some time and which she sat down and waited through. She did not raise her voice. She did not say a single critical word to any member of staff, in front of anybody or privately, then or afterwards, which was not politeness; it was tactics, because a criticism can be answered and absorbed and forgotten, and a question cannot. What she did was ask questions, in a friendly and interested tone, in the presence of the man from the county council who had been telephoned during the eleven minutes and had arrived, and she asked several of them twice, and she used the names of four residents she had met in October, correctly, six weeks later, without notes, and asked where each of them was today. There is no way to be asked that question and not understand what it means.
[/SCENE7]

[SCENE8]
It is important not to overstate what any of that accomplished. There was no inspection, no policy, no headline, nothing in a newspaper, no reform of anything anywhere, and no other home in the country was affected in any way whatsoever. It changed one building, for a while, and one building is not a scandal and does not make a headline and cannot be measured against the size of the problem. The window was repaired, and the first-floor dayroom was redecorated over the winter, and some money that had been going somewhere else went there for a period of about two years, and then, in all probability, things drifted back to roughly where they had been, because that is what happens. That is the realistic scale of it and there is no point pretending otherwise. But the mechanism is worth understanding, because it was the only one she had and she used it for sixteen years: she could not inspect anything, or order anything, or fund anything, or complain about anything to anybody, and she could arrive, unannounced, on an ordinary afternoon, in a place that had spent a week preparing for her the first time. That is a smaller power than people imagine she had and a larger one than it sounds. Institutions changed their behaviour, quietly and at the margins, when they understood that the visit might not be the only visit, and that the person who had come in October had a memory and no diary commitments on a Tuesday afternoon in November. She never explained any of this to anybody and never described it as a strategy, and it worked because it did not look like one. It looked like somebody who had put her hand on a wall in October, and had said nothing at all about it for six weeks, and had not forgotten about it, and had a free Tuesday.
[/SCENE8]
