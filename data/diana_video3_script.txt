The printed schedule for the afternoon of the fourteenth of October, 1985, allowed forty minutes for the market square. It was a typed sheet, four copies, one for the car and three for the men who would be walking behind her, and it had been prepared by people who were good at their jobs and who had built into it the usual margin for a crowd that ran over. Forty minutes was generous. Forty minutes, for a square that size in a town that size on a wet Monday, was more than the job required. The car came in at ten past two. Diana got out on the far side, into rain that had been falling since eleven and had put six hundred umbrellas up along three sides of the square, and she went to the barrier, and the noise came up the way it always did, and she started along the line. Nothing about the first hour was unusual. She was extremely good at this. She had been doing it for four years by then and she had the whole thing down to a set of small physical decisions repeated several hundred times an hour: the weight forward, the head tipped down, the hand out and taken and released before the grip could close. The men behind her kept the pace, which is its own skill and involves walking slowly enough to be behind her and fast enough to be beside her within a second and a half, for four hours, without ever appearing in a photograph. At ten past three they were three quarters of the way down the second side and running twenty minutes over, which was normal and was already in the margin. The square held six hundred people and the schedule held forty minutes, and both of those numbers were about to stop mattering. She did not get back into the car until twenty past six. What happened in between was not in anybody's plan and was never written into any schedule afterwards.
[/SCENE1]

[SCENE2]
A walkabout is a line, and a line has a physics that nobody explains out loud but everybody in the crowd understands within about four minutes of arriving. The front row belongs to whoever got there first and can stand. That means, in practice, the people who could come at nine in the morning and remain upright until the middle of the afternoon on a wet pavement, which is a filter, and the filter has a shape: it selects for the young, the fit, the unencumbered, and the determined. Behind them the second row can see and cannot reach. The third row sees hats, and knows it will see hats, and stays anyway. Beyond that people hold cameras above their heads and go home with a photograph of the back of somebody's coat, and they do not mind especially, because being there was the point. This is not a criticism of anybody, and nobody in that crowd had arranged it or would have defended it. It is simply what happens when six hundred people want the same forty minutes and the only mechanism available for distributing them is who can stand up longest in the rain. The press understood the physics perfectly and worked with it. The photographers set up along the inside of the barrier on the two long sides, because that is where the faces would be, and every picture that ran in a newspaper the following morning was taken from inside a rectangle that covered roughly a third of the square. The other two thirds were, photographically speaking, not there — not hidden, not excluded, simply outside the frame that every lens in the square had agreed on before anybody arrived. And the far end of that square, the fourth side, had no barrier at all, because the council had decided it would not be needed.
[/SCENE2]

[SCENE3]
The fourth side was flat. That was the whole reason anything ended up there. There was a war memorial at that end and a run of paving that the council had relaid the previous summer, and it was the only stretch of the square without a step or a kerb to negotiate, and so at some point during the morning, without anybody deciding it as policy, it had become the place where the wheelchairs went. By one o'clock there were nine of them in a loose row, and behind and among them perhaps thirty more people who could stand but not for four hours: the very old, two women with sticks, a man in his eighties in a folding garden chair somebody had carried down from a flat above a shop. Their families had brought them down early, parked them where the ground was even, and then stood behind them in the rain for hours holding umbrellas at an angle that kept the chairs dry and the umbrella-holder soaked, which is a thing you can see in the two amateur photographs that exist of that end of the square. There was no barrier because there was no crush. There were no photographers because there was nothing to photograph from that angle except the backs of the crowd on the other three sides. A police constable had been posted at the corner, largely to keep the gap clear for an ambulance that would not be needed, and he had told the people nearest him what he had been told himself, which was accurate and which he passed on kindly: that the route went round three sides, and that she would be going back to the car from the corner by the bank, and that they would get a good view of the car. Nobody argued with him and nobody moved, because the alternative was to try to get round to the other three sides and be in the fourth row of a crowd six hundred deep. Several of them had been there since half past nine.
[/SCENE3]

[SCENE4]
She turned at twenty past three. There is no dramatic account of this because there was nothing dramatic in it. She reached the corner where the route was supposed to bend back towards the bank, and she looked down the fourth side — at nine chairs in a row in the wet and perhaps thirty people standing behind them who had understood four hours earlier that this was as close as they were going to get — and she stopped, and she said something to the man immediately behind her that nobody standing more than four feet away could hear, and then she walked into the open end of the square where there was no barrier and no rope and no line to work along. Everything downstream of that had to be rebuilt in about ninety seconds, and none of it had been anybody's fault, because nothing in the plan had been wrong; the plan had simply been written by people looking at a map of a square rather than at a square. Two constables came off the far barrier at a fast walk and started making a space that had not existed a moment earlier. The photographers, who were on the wrong side of a rectangle they had spent two hours setting up in, began moving in a body, which in that weather and on that pavement took time. The man with the schedule in his pocket did the arithmetic and understood immediately that the afternoon was gone, and he was, by his own account many years later, not annoyed but entirely occupied: what he remembered was the logistics of the next three hours and the fact that he did not once think to suggest she come back. By the time any of it caught up with her she was already crouching down at the first chair, out of the rain from nobody's umbrella, getting wet, with the hem of her coat on a paving slab that had four hours of a market day on it.
[/SCENE4]

[SCENE5]
What happens in an encounter like that lasts between ten and thirty seconds and is almost entirely unmemorable to anybody watching. A woman held up a photograph in a plastic wallet, a wedding photograph from 1951, and explained who was in it and which of them were still alive, which took a while, and was listened to. A man had brought flowers that were not for the princess and said so, and asked whether she would mind taking them anyway because his wife had grown them and had died in March and he had not known what else to do with them; and she took them and put them on her arm and carried them for the rest of the afternoon and did not hand them to anybody behind her, which several people noticed. A woman in the third chair said nothing at all, could not, and Diana crouched and held her hand for what one witness put at nearly a minute, which in the arithmetic of that afternoon was an enormous amount of time. There was a man who wanted to complain about a hospital and did, in detail, with dates, and she let him finish, which took most of two minutes and which the two constables standing behind her both later said was the moment they understood the afternoon was not going to be recoverable. There was somebody who asked her nothing and only wanted to say that they had been up since six. None of these people had been on any list. None of them were the reason she was in the town, and none of them appeared in the following morning's newspapers, which ran a photograph taken ninety minutes earlier on the second side of the square, because that was the picture that existed, and a newspaper cannot print a thing that no camera was pointing at.
[/SCENE5]

[SCENE6]
By the time she reached the ninth chair it was ten past five and the light was going and it was still raining. The physical part of this is rarely described because it does not photograph and because complaining about it would have been unthinkable. A hand offered up to be shaken is taken with the fingers, not the palm, and it is taken by somebody who is frequently gripped rather than shaken, held on to, pulled slightly, patted; and it is done two thousand times in an afternoon. She wore rings and by the end of a long day the rings had turned and left a mark across the inside of two fingers, which is the sort of detail that never appears anywhere because it is not interesting and because the person it happened to would have been embarrassed to mention it. The lower back goes first, from crouching and rising and crouching again at the height of a wheelchair arm, and then the jaw, from the particular effort of being warmly and genuinely pleased to see six hundred consecutive strangers while wet. None of this is a hardship on any real scale, and she would have been the first and loudest to say so, and she was standing in front of a woman who could not lift her own hand while it was happening. It is simply the material fact of the work, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative is to describe the afternoon as a gesture, and it was not a gesture. It was four hours of physical labour performed in the rain, at the end of a day that had already contained a factory and a lunch, in a corner of a square where there was no camera to record that she had done it, and where the only people who would ever be able to confirm it were the people it was being done for.
[/SCENE6]

[SCENE7]
She was not able to keep the names. Nobody could; two thousand people in an afternoon is not a memory, it is weather, and anybody who claims otherwise about that kind of day is describing something they have invented afterwards. She knew this perfectly well, and the thing she did about it was not to write anything down and not to have anybody else write it down either. What she did instead only became visible the following spring, in a different town, two hundred miles away, in a market square with a very similar arrangement of barriers on three sides and a flat open fourth side where the same sorting had happened for the same reason. The schedule that day allowed fifty minutes, and had been written, again, by people with a map. She turned at the corner within the first twenty and went to the open end first, before the barriers, before the photographers had settled, at the point in the afternoon when she still had a back and a jaw and the light was good. The men behind her had by then stopped being surprised, and one of them had privately started building the extra time into the plan and not telling anybody where it had gone. It happened again in the June, and in the September after that, and it went on happening for years, in towns whose names are in nobody's account of her life, and at no stage did anyone announce it as a policy or brief a newspaper about it or work it into a speech, because it was not a policy and there was nothing in it to announce. One of the constables from the first afternoon was transferred the following year and told the story in a new station canteen and was not believed. It was a turn at a corner, repeated.

[SCENE8]
The newspapers of the fifteenth of October, 1985, carried a photograph of a young woman in a wet coat laughing at something a man in the front row had said, on the second side of a market square, at about ten to three in the afternoon. It is a good photograph, and it was on several front pages, and the man in the front row who said the funny thing presumably dined out on it for a decade and was entitled to. It is also, in the strict sense, the least interesting thirty seconds of that afternoon, because it is the part that everybody in the square with a camera was already pointing at. The people who had been at the front got the photograph and a fair reason to be pleased with their morning. The nine people in the chairs at the flat end, and the thirty behind them, and the man in the folding garden chair from the flat above the shop, got something with no photograph attached to it at all, which is why the only record of it is what those people told their families that evening and went on telling them, and then what their families told other people afterwards, in the way that a story with no photograph attached to it has to travel if it is going to travel at all. Four hours and ten minutes against a schedule of forty. A route that bent the wrong way at a corner by a bank. Two thousand hands and not one name kept. There is no object to point at here, nothing kept in a drawer, nothing that survived the afternoon except forty or so people's account of twenty seconds each. It is a small enough thing to put next to a life that large, and it is the part that the people who were actually there in the rain never stopped talking about.
